Introduction

What Are We Creating Together?

In 2023, Louis Riel School Division set out a Multi-Year Strategic Plan with a simple conviction at its centre: schools exist to help students, staff, families, and communities flourish.

The plan is organized around four priorities: Belonging, Mastery, Independence, and Generosity. Twenty-five strategic actions give those priorities concrete shape.

The plan did not begin from a blank page. It builds on the foundation of the Multi-Year Strategic Plan 2019–2023, the insights of the Progress Report: Taking Stock of Our MYSP 2019–2023 (March 2023), and three months of community engagement that spring. Taking Stock was not a desk exercise. It drew 7,201 survey responses from students in Grades 5 to 12, roughly 66 per cent of them, 2,013 from staff, roughly 64 per cent including those in casual positions, and 230 from parents and guardians. What they said is why the plan says what it says. It reflects commitments to diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility; Indigenous worldviews; co-stewardship; family partnerships; student voice and agency; and data-informed collaboration. The work is informed by Mamàhtawisiwin: The Wonder We Are Born With, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's Calls to Action 62 to 64 on education, the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, UNESCO's ISEE Assessment (2022), and research partnerships with universities and educational partners.

Across all four priorities, the division applies an anti-racist and anti-oppressive lens. This means examining how systems, structures, and practices create barriers, then working intentionally to remove those barriers so that all learners and staff can belong, grow, and thrive.

One finding runs through the whole of this plan and belongs here rather than at the end of it. The gap between Indigenous and non-Indigenous students in achievement, attendance, and self-reported wellbeing is persistent and, in some cases, widening, even as division-wide averages improve. The next plan has to be built around closing it.

This living record is how we report on that work. It is not a scoreboard. It is a record of what has changed, what we are learning, and what still needs attention. Some sections are deliberately left open. Where we do not yet have something to report, we say so, and we name the work of completing the picture.

Two things sit at the bottom of this page and are worth knowing about before you start. Words We Use explains the school vocabulary that appears here. Tell Us What You Think is how to tell us we have got something wrong.

The Living Record

How we currently understand the work. This page is not another document. It is the current, continuously maintained interpretation of the plan.

Each action is described using three developmental stages:

Emerging

A promise made. The commitment is public and the first work is underway, but too few students would notice it yet. It lives in plans, pilots, and beginnings.

Developing

A promise being kept, in some places more than others. Some students experience this consistently; for others it depends on where they are.

Embedded

A promise woven into everyday practice. The work is load-bearing: stopping it would mean dismantling something. The question now is depth, and who is still left out.

Signs of Change

What Has Changed Since 2023?

Before the action-by-action detail, here are nine signs of change that students, staff, families, and communities can see. These nine were chosen on one test: a student, a family or a staff member can see or feel them without reading a document. Each one is traceable to the strategic actions below.

Kindergarten and BLAST

2023

Full-day Kindergarten operated in only select schools, so many families had to arrange and coordinate coverage for the other half of the day themselves. Where licensed childcare providers were unavailable or could not meet demand, before- and after-school programs were put together school by school, carried by administrators and clerical staff.

2026

Full-Day, Every-Day Kindergarten runs in all 31 LRSD schools that offer Kindergarten, and 71 per cent of the first cohort's Grade 1 teachers saw a positive overall impact. BLAST is now coordinated centrally, running in seven locations serving eight schools, with over 200 students enrolled and more than 250 waiting. An additional location serving two schools opens in September 2026.

These two commitments were taken up in sequence. Full-day Kindergarten in every school closed the half-day gap families had been covering themselves, which brought the next one into focus: a 2025 survey of 2,936 families found more than 1,200 with unmet supervision needs before and after the bell. That demand runs past what licensed childcare providers have capacity to absorb, so the division centralized the effort where the need is greatest. Licensed childcare providers remain essential partners, and reaching half of LRSD communities by January 2027 depends on working with them.

Nutrition

2023

School nutrition depended on school-by-school effort and fundraising.

2026

The Universal Nutrition Program runs from a commissary kitchen at the René Deleurme Centre. Orders grew more than 375 per cent in the first implementation phase and are still climbing year over year.

The program is open to every student, so taking a meal is simply part of the school day. No form, no threshold, no one deciding who qualifies. That is what makes it ordinary, and ordinary is what makes it work. Kitchen capacity has set the pace of expansion, and that capacity is being built.

The order matters. LRSD committed to a universal nutrition program in its 2023 plan and had the commissary kitchen under way before the funding to run it existed. Manitoba introduced its own universal school nutrition program in September 2024, backed by $30 million a year, and budgeted $1,594,000 for this division in 2026–27. That funding did not start the work. It brought a commitment the division had already made to fruition.

Buildings

2023

A second school for the southeast had been requested since 2013, and the community's first school had opened over capacity in 2017. Eleven schools had no air conditioning, a locally funded retrofit begun in 2015 having stalled after five.

2026

The new school opened in September 2025, on the schedule set when its design was authorized three years earlier. A six-year plan to retrofit ten schools with air conditioning is under way, funded locally, and controlled entry systems are being installed division-wide.

None of this arrived on its own. The new school took twelve years from first request to opening, and assertive, unrelenting advocacy at the end, when approval of the final tender stalled with the building half up. Air conditioning and controlled access are being paid for locally. LRSD 2034 now sets the ten-year infrastructure ask in priority order, and the Annual School Building Review Dashboard publishes the condition of every school. The division cannot build faster than the Province funds. It can say plainly what it needs, in what order, and why.

Early Screening

2023

Early identification of learning needs varied from school to school.

2026

Universal literacy screening runs in Kindergarten and Grade 1, progress monitoring continues to Grade 8, and numeracy screening runs to Grade 4, in both the English and French Immersion programs.

Prevention now starts with the same information for every child. That is the gain, and it carries a risk worth naming: a screen given to everyone can quietly become a way of sorting them. A result below benchmark tells a teacher where to look next. It is not a verdict on a child, and keeping it from becoming one is part of the work.

French Immersion

2023

Middle Immersion ran in two schools, École Julie-Riel in the south and École Henri-Bergeron in the north. There was no Late Immersion pathway.

2026

Middle Immersion runs in three schools. A program opened in September 2025 at École Mazina-Giizhik, which welcomed 815 students from Kindergarten to Grade 8 in its first year, and Late Immersion opened the same month at École Van Belleghem. The first cohort in each program finished the year using French confidently.

Manitoba recognizes three entry points into French Immersion, and few divisions offer more than the earliest. LRSD now offers all three. Offering all three entry points broadens who can begin French Immersion. Sustaining students through to graduation is the division's next responsibility, and that question comes into focus as the first cohorts join the regular stream in Grades 7 and 9, respectively.

Indigenous Learning

2023

Access to Indigenous language classes, land-based learning, and Indigenous ways of knowing varied from school to school.

2026

Every student from Kindergarten to Grade 2 now receives Indigenous language exposure through a universal program, expanding to include Cree and Michif alongside Ojibwe. Grades 9 to 12 language options are growing, and land-based inquiry and Treaty learning are more visible across schools. In October 2025, the whole division set aside a day for Treaty education, with staff gathered at the RBC Convention Centre in the morning and back in their schools for the afternoon.

Language carries culture, identity, and ways of understanding the world. Expanding Indigenous language, land-based, and Treaty learning is how reconciliation becomes something students live, not only something adults discuss. This expansion is substantial, and there remains meaningful distance between where the division is now and what the commitment asks of it.

Responding to Distress

2023

How schools responded to students in distress varied school by school, strategy by strategy.

2026

TCIS is now the single trauma-responsive standard across all 41 schools, delivered by an internal network of 44 trainers drawn from clinicians, student services teachers and administrators. All leaders and clinicians, all educational assistants and student services teachers, and all classroom teachers have received training.

Staff across the division now share a common language and approach for the most difficult moments of the school day. Trained everywhere and practised everywhere are different milestones, and only the first one is finished.

System-Wide Reflection

What We Are Learning

Individual actions tell individual stories. Read together, they reveal patterns. Eight stand out.

Removing the eligibility test is what changes who comes forward.

Universal design does more than widen eligibility. Universal full-day Kindergarten, universal nutrition, universal screening, and BLAST all demonstrate the same pattern. Designing for everyone removes the step of determining who qualifies, along with the judgement that step can carry, and it opens participation to families who would not otherwise have come forward. The division's task then becomes making sure everyone can take part, rather than identifying who is eligible.

Drawn from: Actions 2.3, 4.3, 4.4

Supporting students begins with supporting staff.

Student learning and wellbeing are closely connected to the learning, wellbeing, and confidence of the adults around them. Across staff wellbeing, employment equity, responsive and appropriate supports, screening practices, transitions planning, trauma-responsive practice, and social-emotional learning, the same lesson emerges: adults cannot consistently provide what they have not had the opportunity to learn, experience, and practice themselves. Building staff capacity is not separate from improving student outcomes. It is one of the primary ways those outcomes improve.

Drawn from: Actions 1.2, 1.3, 1.5, 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 3.4, 4.6

Good ideas need systems behind them.

Many initiatives begin with vision, energy, and commitment. Lasting change, however, depends on the structures, processes, tools, and shared practices that allow the work to continue and grow over time. Progress in areas such as employment equity, responsive and appropriate supports, universal screening, social-emotional learning, data stewardship, pathways planning, and nutrition demonstrates the importance of building strong foundations. Sustainable improvement depends on both innovation and infrastructure. Nutrition is the plainest case: the promise to feed any student who wants a meal could not scale until there was a commissary kitchen at the René Deleurme Centre to cook in.

Drawn from: Actions 1.5, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 3.3, 3.4, 3.6, 4.4, 4.6

Change takes time to become everyday practice.

New ideas can be widely supported and still not yet be part of daily routines. Across staff wellbeing, trauma-responsive schools, employment equity, responsive and appropriate supports, social-emotional learning, screening, data stewardship, and learning with the land, the same lesson appears: adopting something is not the same as embedding it. Most of the work of improvement happens between implementation and permanence, after a decision has been made and before it has become ordinary practice, and it includes building the means to measure whether the change is having its intended impact.

Drawn from: Actions 1.2, 1.3, 1.5, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 3.3, 4.5, 4.6

The work that reached furthest is work the division could not have done alone.

Many of the most significant changes in this plan came from partnerships rather than from programs. Families, students, staff, community organizations, Elders, Knowledge Keepers, volunteers, university partners, and public agencies each supplied something the division did not hold on its own: knowledge, standing in a community, or simply the hands to do the work. Whether the work focused on wellbeing, inclusion, active transportation, or learning with the land, it depended on people outside the organization choosing to carry it too. Universities are part of that picture. Early screening has developed over time and continues to evolve through close partnerships with researchers at the University of Toronto's Ontario Institute for Studies in Education, on literacy, and at Carleton University, on numeracy. The trauma-responsive model the division adopted comes from Cornell University, and the feasibility work on a certification pathway for French-speaking educational assistants was carried out with Université de Saint-Boniface and the Bureau de l'éducation française.

Drawn from: Actions 1.1, 1.2, 2.2, 2.4, 4.2, 4.4, 4.5

People sustain what they help create.

Many of LRSD's most significant innovations have been developed by staff working and learning together. Internal trauma-responsive training, universal screening processes, a framework for resource allocation and programming to support student inclusion and diverse needs, data stewardship practices, nutrition expansion, BLAST, and learning-with-the-land initiatives all reflect a consistent pattern of local design and collective problem-solving. When people help create a solution, they are more likely to understand it, improve it, and sustain it over time. Trauma-responsive practice shows it most plainly: forty-four staff now train their own colleagues, so the approach no longer depends on anyone being brought in from outside to teach it.

Drawn from: Actions 1.3, 1.5, 2.3, 2.4, 3.3, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

A practice can be established well before its effect can be measured.

A practice can be fully established before the means of checking it exist. Employment equity is the clearest case: bias-in-hiring training, standardized interview questions, and revised hiring protocols are all in place, while ethnocultural information is not yet collected through employee profiles or applications, so the division cannot yet say whether its workforce is coming to reflect the students it serves. Screening shows a gentler version of the same pattern: the instruments and the reporting exist, and what remains is the school-based routine that turns a result into a change in teaching that improves learning outcomes. Building the measure alongside the practice is what allows a promise to be verified rather than only described.

Drawn from: Actions 2.3, 4.6

Progress matters, but not everyone is benefiting equally.

Division-wide results show encouraging progress in many areas of student learning and wellbeing. However, Indigenous students continue to experience lower achievement, lower attendance, and less positive wellbeing outcomes than non-Indigenous students. Improving overall results is important, but it is not enough. An honest account of this plan holds both facts at once, and the next plan must be built around closing that gap, not around the averages.

Drawn from: division-wide disaggregated data, Annual Report on Continuous Improvement, November 2025

One question sits underneath all eight of these patterns. The gap between Indigenous and non-Indigenous students remains persistent and, in some cases, continues to widen while division-wide averages improve. The division does not read that as an unexplained result. It is what colonial systems produce when they are left to operate as they were built to operate, and it is why this plan applies an anti-racist and anti-oppressive lens rather than treating the gap as a question of programming alone. Honouring Treaty requires dismantling those systems, not only working around them, and the division said so to all staff at its division-wide Treaty Education Learning Journey in October 2025. What the division still cannot say is which of the actions described on this page is changing that reality, and by how much. Naming the cause is not the same as measuring the remedy.

These patterns were drawn from the action records on this page. They still need to be tested with program leads before informing the 2027–2031 planning cycle.

The next Multi-Year Strategic Plan will be shaped with students, staff, families, Elders and Knowledge Keepers, and the wider community. This page is one of its starting points.

Turning Data into Action in Schools

This year, schools were invited to participate in a collaborative process to explore staff wellbeing, with 17 schools taking part. In partnership with divisional staff, principals supported conversations with their teams to reflect on their experiences and identify what was most important to them now.

Rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach, schools were supported in curating their wellbeing work based on their specific context, priorities, and staff feedback. Guiding the work were several key principles, including acknowledging current realities, engaging staff in planning, and focusing on meaningful, system-level change.

What the Work Looked Like in Schools

Across participating schools, the work was shaped by local priorities, but shared common elements grounded in reflection, collaboration, and relationship-building.

In some schools, staff participated in strengths-based team activities, exploring how individual strengths contribute to team dynamics and how differences can sometimes create both energy and tension. Other schools chose to revisit and build on the original Guarding Minds data: through facilitated sessions, staff shared their current perspectives, and that feedback was analyzed and returned to school leadership to inform planning.

In all cases, the process emphasized creating space for honest dialogue, reflection, and collective problem-solving. Importantly, schools were encouraged to see wellbeing not as an initiative, but as something embedded in all aspects of school planning and daily practice.

Strengthening Key Staff Supports

A focus of this work has been collaboration with the School and Classroom Support Team (SCST), which brings together the Instructional Support Team, the Indigenous Education and Languages Team, and the Clinical Services Team. Guarding Minds data suggested that some of these staff may face unique challenges, prompting further exploration.

This work has focused on understanding the complexity of these roles, gathering input from staff across disciplines, and working collaboratively with senior leadership to identify both short- and long-term actions. The goal is to ensure that these critical staff, who play an essential role in supporting students, feel supported, valued, and equipped to succeed in their work.

Early Signs of Impact

Many participating schools are incorporating wellbeing into their planning for the upcoming school year, identifying specific goals and strategies to strengthen staff experiences. Across the division, there is growing awareness of the importance of workplace culture and the role it plays in supporting staff and students. There are also signs of shifting mindsets, with leaders and staff increasingly recognizing the importance of collective approaches and the role of workplace conditions in shaping wellbeing.

The School and Classroom Support Team's own wellbeing action plan is the clearest example. Built with staff and now delivering, its first short-term actions on identification and access, including staff badges, fobs and parking clarity, were complete or nearly complete by June 2026.

What we are learning

Wellbeing is shifting from an individual-strategies model, pointing staff to assistance programs, toward the conditions people actually work in. In seventeen schools this year that meant facilitated conversations in which staff named what mattered most to them now, with the feedback returned to school leadership to shape planning. The division's own framing captures the finding: wellbeing is not an initiative but something embedded in planning and daily practice.

The seventeen schools also produced a finding none of them could have reached alone. Schools with very different contexts named the same conditions: isolation, onboarding, staff dynamics, and staff wanting consistent and predictable responses to student behaviour. School leaders also named a gap between the support they intended to give and the support staff experienced.

What needs attention

The baseline itself is the open question. Guarding Minds was run in February 2024 and everything since has been built on it, but two things now sit against it. School leaders who took part this year said the results no longer reflected where their schools actually were, and a divisional focus group process in March 2026, which reached 60 staff across School and Classroom Support Team and Clinical Services roles, found that concerns around involvement and influence, psychological and social support, and workload had if anything intensified since the survey. Staff in that process also described a reluctance to speak up. A wellbeing program that cannot hear bad news is not measuring wellbeing. Deciding whether to refresh the baseline, replace the instrument, or measure differently is work for the incoming leadership team.

Behaviour Is Communication

Therapeutic Crisis Intervention for Schools (TCIS), developed by Cornell University, is the division-wide trauma-responsive model. It is grounded in a simple idea: behaviour is a form of communication. When students are overwhelmed, dysregulated, or struggling, their actions are often expressing a need. Staff learn to ask not “What’s wrong?” but “What does this student need right now?”

One Framework, Every School

Rather than relying on isolated strategies or one-time training, LRSD adapted a common framework that can support every student, in every school, through a trauma-informed lens. A committee of teachers, clinicians, and school leaders defined the professional learning framework, and an internal network of trainers carries it into classrooms, hallways, and everyday interactions.

Starting From the Student

One-size-fits-all instruction fits almost no one. A learner-centred approach starts instructional planning from the student: their needs, interests, aptitudes, and abilities. It is also asset-based. Rather than cataloguing deficits, teachers identify what a student already brings and build from there. Engagement and belonging follow strength, not remediation.

Resource Allocation, Made Visible

The Resource Allocation and Planning Framework (RAF) documents every adult support a student requires, including instructional supports that fall outside formal plans. That breadth matters: it shows how support is distributed across staff roles and helps schools avoid defaulting to educational assistants as the primary support mechanism.

For planning, RAF data is translated into two complementary measures. The Cumulative Weighted Score accounts for all identified supports and gives a divisional view of overall support intensity, grouped into three bands: Intensive, Moderate, and Minimal. The Complexity Score uses the most intensive support identified for each student, and aggregates to the class, school, and divisional levels. The bands describe the level of support provided, not characteristics of individual students. The result is staffing and class composition decisions that rest on shared evidence.

Information Literacy, K to 12

A cohort of teacher-librarians, working in three groups across the early years, middle years and high school, is defining the Kindergarten to Grade 12 information literacy competencies students need, aligned with provincial curricula, and embedding them in classroom teaching as instructional partners. The continuum covers more than research and evaluation. It also covers how students communicate online, how they respond when someone is struggling, and what to do when something goes wrong. What this looks like in a classroom varies by age and is recognizably the same skill. In the middle years, a Red Flag Challenge asks Grade 5 and 6 students to judge whether a described online behaviour is safe, name the risk, and propose a safer alternative. What we are learning

The order of the work turned out to matter. The division built the policy, the training and the hiring practices first, and is only now building the means to tell whether any of it is changing who gets hired. Establishing the measure alongside the practices would have been the stronger sequence, because a commitment about representation needs a count from the outset rather than once the practices are settled.

The second thing we have learned is why that count is hard. The Ethnocultural Survey was developed with staff, with members of racialized communities and with each of the division's unions, and it is voluntary. Self-identification runs on trust rather than on a field in a form. A person decides whether to tell an employer who they are based on what they expect the employer will do with it, which means the measurement problem and the equity problem are the same problem.

What needs attention

The division cannot yet say whether its workforce is coming to reflect the students it serves. Ethnocultural data is not attached to employee profiles or applications, so the measurement that would answer the question does not exist. That is the real limit on this action. The policies, the training and the hiring practices are in place. The outcome they exist to produce is unmeasured, and until it is measured this stays a promise being kept in some places more than others.

What we are learning

The learning here is about the distance between a commitment and a first step. Four years in, the most substantial thing this action has produced is a study, and the study is about employees rather than students. That is still meaningful groundwork. A French-speaking educational assistant who already works in our schools, already knows our students and already speaks the language of instruction is the shortest route into a French Immersion classroom that exists, and starting there is sensible.

But it is a different promise from the one the plan made, and that difference should be visible rather than smoothed over. What the study actually found has not been shared, so this card cannot say what barriers those educational assistants face. Until it is, the honest description of this action is that it has a funded beginning and a set of intentions that do not yet form one pathway: an Educational Assistant diploma for Grade 11 and 12 students, information evenings, alumni tracking and a CATEP connection are four starting points rather than a single route a young person could follow.

Two pathways have been running for years and belong in this account. The Community-based Aboriginal Teacher Education Program is a joint venture between the University of Winnipeg’s Faculty of Education, Manitoba Entrepreneurship, Training and Trade, and partner school divisions. It lets Indigenous educational assistants earn an integrated Bachelor of Arts and Bachelor of Education while working full-time in an LRSD school: classes in the evenings from September to April, released to the university and to full-time student teaching from May to July, about six years in all. An LRSD educational assistant completed it in 2023 and took a Grade 2 and 3 teaching position in the division that June. The Immigrant Teacher Education Program, a joint venture between the University of Winnipeg’s Faculty of Education and partner school divisions, does the same work for internationally trained teachers who have not been able to establish themselves in a Manitoba classroom. LRSD’s partnership with CATEP continues. Its partnership with ITEP has not been sustained. Neither is enough on its own to answer the promise this action makes.

What needs attention

This is a funded feasibility study, not yet a program. Nothing here should be read as evidence that the program itself is succeeding, because it doesn't exist yet.

The second gap is worth naming just as plainly. The 2023 plan commits to a program for students from historically oppressed, marginalized and underrepresented groups. The funded work so far is a feasibility study for current employees, specifically French-speaking educational assistants. Both matter, and they are not the same promise. The student-facing program still needs to be designed, and no date has been set for a first cohort. That decision belongs to the incoming leadership team.

Ongoing

  • Launch post-secondary program for French-speaking educational assistants in partnership with Université de Saint-Boniface and Bureau de l'éducation française.
  • Collaborate with Université de Saint-Boniface to offer Grade 11 and 12 students opportunity to obtain their Educational Assistant Diploma.
  • Host annual information evenings to engage and encourage high school students to pursue studies in Faculty of Education.

Looking Ahead

The Multi-Year Strategic Plan is not a project with an end date. It is part of an ongoing effort to create conditions in which students, staff, families, and communities can flourish.

Three questions come out of this cycle rather than out of the idea of planning, and they are the ones worth carrying into the next one:

  • Where have we trained without embedding? Several actions on this page have the professional learning in place and not yet the daily routine that would make it stick.
  • Where have universal approaches still left particular groups behind? Making something available to everyone is not the same as it reaching everyone.
  • What took longer than the plan expected, and what does that tell us about how to design the next one?

Plain Language

Words We Use

← Back to where you were

Schools have their own vocabulary, and it can shut people out. Some of it is on this page because it is the official wording of the plan, and some because it is genuinely the clearest word for the thing. Either way, nobody should have to already work here to read this. If a word on this page is not explained below, that is a gap and we would like to hear about it.

Words about this plan
School division
The public body that runs a group of schools in a defined area. LRSD runs 41 schools in south-east Winnipeg.
Trustee
A person elected by the community to govern the school division. LRSD has nine, elected every four years alongside the Winnipeg civic election.
Divisional, division-wide
Applying to all LRSD schools rather than to one school.
Multi-Year Strategic Plan (MYSP)
The four-year plan that says what the division is trying to do and how it will know whether it is working. The current one runs 2023 to 2027.
Priority
One of the four big commitments the plan is organized around: Belonging, Mastery, Independence and Generosity.
Belonging, Mastery, Independence, Generosity
The plan’s four priorities, and also the four needs at the centre of the Circle of Courage, a model of healthy development drawn from Indigenous child-rearing philosophy. Belonging is being known and welcome somewhere. Mastery is getting good at something. Independence is having a say in your own life. Generosity is having something worth giving.
Co-stewardship
Named in the plan as a commitment to all staff. It means the people who do a piece of work have a real say in how it is decided and how it changes, rather than being told afterwards.
Anti-racist, anti-oppressive
Anti-racist means actively working to remove racism from how a system operates, which is more than avoiding racist acts. The division’s 2022 report Creating a Culture of Diversity, Equity, Inclusion and Anti-Racism describes what this looked like under the previous plan. Anti-oppressive applies the same test to every form of exclusion the plan names, including sexism, classism, ableism and heterosexism. Both describe something an organization does rather than something it believes.
IBPOC
Indigenous, Black and People of Colour. Used on this page when reporting how students describe themselves.
Strategic action
One of the 25 specific promises inside the plan. Each one has its own card on this page.
Emerging, Developing, Embedded
The three stages used on this page to describe how far along an action is. Full definitions are in the introduction at the top.
Data Hub
A public LRSD website holding twelve dashboards of divisional data, such as attendance, enrolment and provincial assessment results.
Annual Report to the Community (ARC)
The division's yearly account of its work, published each summer. Past reports are linked at the top of this page.
Street data
Evidence gathered close to the ground: what students, families and staff say about their own experience, collected by the people who work alongside them every day. It is treated here as primary evidence, not as anecdote filling space until numbers arrive.
Asset mapping
Beginning from what a community already has, including its people, organizations, spaces and skills, rather than from what it lacks, and recording it so the work can build on it.
Words about teaching and learning
Curriculum
What students are expected to learn in a subject. In Manitoba it is set by the province.
Outcomes
In a school context this usually means the specific things a curriculum says students should be able to do, not results.
Assessment
Any way a teacher finds out what a student has learned.
Formative and summative assessment
Formative happens during learning and is meant to guide the next lesson. Summative happens at the end and is meant to sum up what was learned. Both can be outcomes-based.
Outcomes-based assessment
Judging a student against what the curriculum says they should be able to do, rather than against an average of the marks they collected along the way.
Dynamic assessment
Assessment that changes as the student does. What counts is the most recent and most consistent evidence of what a student can do now, not their first attempt.
Screening
A short, standard check given to every child at the same point, to spot who may need extra help. It is not a diagnosis and it is not a mark.
Progress monitoring
Repeating a short check over time to see whether the support a student is getting is working.
Benchmark
The level a check expects for a child of that age. A result below the benchmark is a flag to look more closely, not a label.
Universal
Offered to everyone, without an application, a fee or a test of need.
Differentiated instruction
Teaching the same content in more than one way in the same classroom, so it reaches students who learn differently.
Professional learning
Training and study for staff. Often called professional development (PD).
Reframing
Used on this page in a specific sense: changing what sits at the centre of a course rather than adding to it. In Grade 11 English and History it means Indigenous stories, histories and ways of seeing the world become the material of the course itself.
Land-based learning
Learning that happens on the land and treats the land itself as a source of knowledge, guided by Elders, Knowledge Keepers and community.
Elder and Knowledge Keeper
People recognized by their communities as carriers of Indigenous knowledge, language and teachings. The two terms are not interchangeable and communities decide who holds each role.
Two-Eyed Seeing
Learning to see with the strengths of Indigenous knowledge in one eye and the strengths of Western knowledge in the other, and to use both together rather than choosing between them. The term comes from Mi’kmaw Elder Albert Marshall, who named it Etuaptmumk.
Culturally sustaining pedagogy
Teaching that treats a student’s language, culture and community as something to keep going, rather than as something to move past on the way to school success.
Translanguaging
Letting a student use every language they have while they learn, instead of requiring them to work only in the language of instruction. A student might reason through a problem in one language and write the answer in another.
Asset-based
Starting from what a student, family or community already has and can do, rather than from what is missing. The opposite is deficit-based.
Early, Middle and Senior Years
Manitoba's names for Kindergarten to Grade 4, Grades 5 to 8, and Grades 9 to 12.
Practical Arts
Hands-on courses. Industrial Arts includes woods, metals and construction. Human Ecology includes foods, textiles and family studies.
Project-based learning
Learning organized around a real project that runs for weeks, often earning credit in more than one subject at once.
Credit
What a student earns for completing a high school course. Thirty credits are needed to graduate in Manitoba.
Entry point
A grade at which a student can start French Immersion. Manitoba recognizes three: Kindergarten, Grade 4 and Grade 6 (or 7).
Single-track and dual-track
A single-track school offers one program. A dual-track school offers both the English program and French Immersion in the same building.
Words about supports for students
Regular school
The school a student normally attends. Some documents call this a student's home school, which is not the same thing as homeschooling.
Educational assistant
A staff member who works alongside teachers to support students directly in the classroom.
Student services teacher
A teacher whose role is to help plan and coordinate supports for students who need them.
Clinician
A specialist employed by the division, such as a speech-language pathologist, occupational therapist, physiotherapist, social worker or school psychologist.
Tiers of support
A common way of describing the degree to which student support is specialized. Tier 1 is good teaching for everyone. Tier 2 adds small-group help. Tier 3 adds intensive individual help.
Trauma-responsive
An approach that assumes difficult behaviour is often communicating an unmet need, and responds with connection rather than punishment.
Regulation and co-regulation
Regulation is a person's ability to manage strong feelings. Co-regulation is a calm adult helping a student get there. Neither has anything to do with rules.
Dropping the rope
TCIS language for a choice an adult makes during a conflict. If a student is pulling hard on one end of a rope, an adult who keeps pulling makes the struggle bigger. Dropping the rope means letting go of the argument itself, so there is nothing left to pull against, and the adult is free to attend to what the student actually needs.
Neurodiversity
The fact that people's brains work differently from one another, including autistic people and people with ADHD or learning disabilities, and that this is variation rather than deficit.
Multilingual learner
A student who speaks one or more languages other than the language of instruction. Support for these students is often labelled EAL, English as an Additional Language.
Individual Education Plan (IEP)
A written plan describing a student's goals and the supports they will receive.
Building capacity
Increasing the skill and confidence of staff so they can do the work themselves, rather than adding more specialists. It is not about how many people fit in a building.
Ethic of Hospitality
The principle guiding how the division receives newcomer students and families: belonging and safety come before process, and what a family arrives with counts before anything they are said to lack. The phrase entered LRSD through The Ethics of Hospitality through an Anti-Racist Perspective, a 2022 session led by a member of the division’s Indigenous Council of Grandmothers and Grandfathers with divisional principals and students, which asked leaders to consider hospitality when decolonizing educational practice. Described in Creating a Culture of Diversity, Equity, Inclusion and Anti-Racism (2022).
Wraparound support
Several services working together around one student or family instead of separately.
Digital first-aid
Knowing what to do in the moment when something goes wrong online: who to tell, what to keep, and what not to do next.
Transitions
Moving from one setting to the next: elementary to middle school, middle to high school, or high school to adulthood, including work or further study.
